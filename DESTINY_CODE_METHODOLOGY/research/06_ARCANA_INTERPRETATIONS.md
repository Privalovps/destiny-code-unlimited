# Original English archetype library

All prose below is editorial synthesis of general source concepts. It is not a translation, a copy of a proprietary reading, or a clinically validated assessment. Domain adaptations are explicitly original. Stable IDs, page ranges and position rules are in data/arcana.json.

Numbering: 8 = Justice, 11 = Strength, 22 = The Fool. Death and The Devil are retained as archetypal names; neither predicts literal death nor establishes moral character.

## 1. The Magician

**Core:** initiative and authorship. **Themes:** initiative, agency, originality.

**Constructive:** Turn an idea into a first working attempt; act without waiting for unanimous approval.

**Shadow:** Treating originality as superiority, or abandoning a project when being a beginner feels uncomfortable.

**Talents:** Prototyping, initiating a project, and making a clear proposal.

**Tension:** Wanting autonomy while needing reassurance that your idea matters.

**Relationships:** Keep your independence while allowing another person to influence the plan.

**Communication:** Say what you intend to do and invite a specific contribution.

**Resources:** Value created through initiative still needs a workable price, delivery process, and follow-through.

**Family:** Try a different approach without treating your family as less capable.

**Growth:** Finish a small promise before announcing a bigger one.

**Example:** You turn a vague group idea into a small prototype, then ask the group to improve it.

**Practice:** Choose one idea and define a deliverable you can finish this week.

**Career settings to explore:** Product development, entrepreneurship, independent creative work..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 117–117, B PDF pp. 105–105, C PDF pp. 44–47.

## 2. The High Priestess

**Core:** receptivity and discernment. **Themes:** receptivity, discernment, privacy.

**Constructive:** Notice what is unsaid, listen carefully, and allow competing perspectives to become clear.

**Shadow:** Hiding a preference behind neutrality, passing information indirectly, or waiting for certainty indefinitely.

**Talents:** Listening, mediation, research interviews, and observing patterns.

**Tension:** Wanting harmony while withholding information needed to create it.

**Relationships:** Respect privacy while making your needs understandable to your partner.

**Communication:** Check an impression with a direct question instead of assuming you know another person's intention.

**Resources:** Careful observation can inform resource decisions; an intuition alone does not establish a fact.

**Family:** Notice an unspoken rule and decide whether to name it openly.

**Growth:** Separate what you observed from what you inferred.

**Example:** You notice a colleague becoming quiet and ask what changed rather than speaking for them.

**Practice:** Write an observation, an interpretation, and one question that could distinguish them.

**Career settings to explore:** Research, mediation, advisory support..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 118–118, B PDF pp. 106–106, C PDF pp. 48–50.

## 3. The Empress

**Core:** cultivation and tangible care. **Themes:** cultivation, care, resources.

**Constructive:** Develop an idea, environment, or relationship through consistent attention and practical support.

**Shadow:** Measuring care by possessions, overmanaging people, or giving so much that appreciation becomes a demand.

**Talents:** Design, hospitality, cultivation, and turning resources into a usable experience.

**Tension:** Wanting to nurture while wanting the result to follow your exact design.

**Relationships:** Make affection tangible while leaving the other person free to choose.

**Communication:** Ask what support would actually help before organizing it for someone else.

**Resources:** Comfort and quality can be worthwhile uses of money; status is a poor substitute for deciding what is enough.

**Family:** Distinguish nurturing a relative from managing that person's choices.

**Growth:** Let the recipient define whether your help is useful.

**Example:** You organize a welcoming dinner, then let guests contribute rather than controlling every detail.

**Practice:** Offer one useful form of care and one genuine choice to the recipient.

**Career settings to explore:** Design, hospitality, product experience, resource coordination..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 119–120, B PDF pp. 107–107, C PDF pp. 51–54.

## 4. The Emperor

**Core:** structure and responsibility. **Themes:** structure, responsibility, authority.

**Constructive:** Create reliable agreements, assign ownership, and take responsibility for decisions.

**Shadow:** Confusing authority with control, refusing to delegate, or avoiding responsibility when outcomes are uncertain.

**Talents:** Operations, planning, leadership, and building repeatable systems.

**Tension:** Wanting everyone to be responsible while denying them decision-making power.

**Relationships:** Reliability matters when it supports mutual choice rather than supervision.

**Communication:** State the agreement, owner, and boundary without turning a preference into an order.

**Resources:** Structure can help resources serve a goal; control over every expense can also become a source of conflict.

**Family:** Examine who was allowed to decide and how responsibility was distributed.

**Growth:** Delegate a real decision and accept a result that differs from your preference.

**Example:** You clarify who owns each task and stop checking work that another person can handle.

**Practice:** Rewrite one recurring conflict as an agreement with clear responsibilities.

**Career settings to explore:** Operations, project leadership, administration..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 121–121, B PDF pp. 108–108, C PDF pp. 55–59.

## 5. The Hierophant

**Core:** learning and transmission. **Themes:** learning, tradition, transmission.

**Constructive:** Understand a principle deeply enough to explain it, test it, and pass it on responsibly.

**Shadow:** Using a rule to end discussion, collecting credentials without practice, or teaching before listening.

**Talents:** Teaching, explaining complex ideas, and preserving useful knowledge.

**Tension:** Wanting to guide others while resisting being corrected yourself.

**Relationships:** Discuss shared values rather than assuming a good relationship follows a single prescribed form.

**Communication:** Explain why a rule exists and ask whether it still serves its purpose.

**Resources:** Knowledge can become a useful service when it solves a real problem; authority alone does not establish value.

**Family:** Keep a tradition because it serves people, rather than because disagreement feels disloyal.

**Growth:** Update one belief when evidence or experience challenges it.

**Example:** You show a new colleague how a process works, then ask whether they see a better way.

**Practice:** Teach one useful idea and record one thing the learner teaches you.

**Career settings to explore:** Education, training, documentation, institutional work..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 122–123, B PDF pp. 109–109, C PDF pp. 60–64.

## 6. The Lovers

**Core:** connection and deliberate choice. **Themes:** connection, choice, beauty.

**Constructive:** Build rapport, appreciate beauty, and make choices that fit your actual values.

**Shadow:** Letting approval decide for you, idealizing a partner, or postponing a choice to avoid disappointment.

**Talents:** Relationship building, collaboration, aesthetics, and understanding preferences.

**Tension:** Wanting genuine closeness while hiding disagreement to preserve a perfect image.

**Relationships:** Choose reciprocal connection over performing an ideal version of yourself.

**Communication:** Name a preference kindly even when someone may want something else.

**Resources:** A pleasing offer still needs a clear decision about costs and commitments.

**Family:** Notice when keeping everyone pleased replaces an honest conversation.

**Growth:** Make a choice you can explain without referring to other people's approval.

**Example:** You tell a friend which plan you prefer and remain open to their different preference.

**Practice:** Choose between two real options using three values that matter to you.

**Career settings to explore:** Client relationships, collaboration, aesthetic services..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 124–124, B PDF pp. 110–111, C PDF pp. 65–68.

## 7. The Chariot

**Core:** direction and coordinated movement. **Themes:** direction, movement, execution.

**Constructive:** Set a destination, organize effort, and help a group move toward a concrete goal.

**Shadow:** Rushing, treating cooperation as a contest, or starting several pursuits without finishing one.

**Talents:** Execution, logistics, motivation, and coordinating action.

**Tension:** Wanting teamwork while insisting on controlling the route and speed.

**Relationships:** A shared direction works best when both people help choose it.

**Communication:** Make the next action clear and check that others consent to the pace.

**Resources:** Progress needs priorities and limits; constant motion does not automatically create value.

**Family:** Notice whether achievement became the only accepted way to earn recognition.

**Growth:** Reduce the number of simultaneous goals and finish one stage.

**Example:** You turn an ambitious launch into three stages and ask the team to agree on the first.

**Practice:** Define one destination, one next step, and one stopping condition.

**Career settings to explore:** Logistics, project delivery, transport, team coordination..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 125–125, B PDF pp. 112–112, C PDF pp. 69–73.

## 8. Justice

**Core:** fair exchange and accountability. **Themes:** accountability, balance, agreements.

**Constructive:** Clarify agreements, compare actions with standards, and take responsibility for your part.

**Shadow:** Keeping score in every interaction, assuming all hardship is deserved, or making judgment a substitute for understanding.

**Talents:** Reviewing details, evaluating agreements, and tracing consequences.

**Tension:** Wanting balanced treatment while overlooking context that makes equal treatment insufficient.

**Relationships:** Agree on what fairness means instead of expecting identical contributions in every situation.

**Communication:** Separate the observable action from a judgment about a person's character.

**Resources:** Clear terms, records, and reciprocal obligations are useful themes; the number predicts no legal outcome.

**Family:** Question a family rule about fairness without assigning blame through the chart.

**Growth:** Repair a specific action instead of proving that you are the fairer person.

**Example:** You resolve a workload dispute by comparing agreed responsibilities with actual tasks.

**Practice:** Choose one agreement and make its expectations explicit for everyone involved.

**Career settings to explore:** Quality review, contracts administration, compliance support..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 126–126, B PDF pp. 113–113, C PDF pp. 74–78.

## 9. The Hermit

**Core:** depth and independent understanding. **Themes:** depth, knowledge, solitude.

**Constructive:** Study patiently, form a considered view, and offer knowledge without needing constant attention.

**Shadow:** Withdrawing before others can know you, using expertise to dismiss them, or waiting for complete knowledge before contributing.

**Talents:** Analysis, long-form study, specialist craft, and thoughtful mentoring.

**Tension:** Wanting to be understood while keeping the process of understanding private.

**Relationships:** Make room for both solitude and dependable contact.

**Communication:** Share the reasoning behind your conclusion rather than only the conclusion.

**Resources:** Depth can support valuable work when others can understand and use it.

**Family:** Notice whether needing space was mistaken for rejection, or silence for agreement.

**Growth:** Bring one private insight into a conversation where it can help.

**Example:** You spend time studying a problem, then give a colleague a short and usable explanation.

**Practice:** Set a study period and a specific time to share what you learned.

**Career settings to explore:** Research, writing, specialist analysis, mentoring..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 127–128, B PDF pp. 114–114, C PDF pp. 79–83.

## 10. The Wheel of Fortune

**Core:** cycles and responsive opportunity. **Themes:** cycles, adaptation, opportunity.

**Constructive:** Notice changing conditions, cooperate with available openings, and adjust without losing your direction.

**Shadow:** Waiting for luck to do the work, changing plans at every fluctuation, or avoiding commitments in pursuit of ease.

**Talents:** Adaptation, connecting opportunities, and responding to a changing environment.

**Tension:** Wanting freedom from rigid schedules while relying on other people's reliability.

**Relationships:** Leave room for spontaneity while keeping the promises that make trust possible.

**Communication:** Discuss a change of plan before assuming others will move with you.

**Resources:** Opportunities are uncertain; define what you can afford before acting on an attractive opening.

**Family:** Identify which routines help and which merely repeat themselves.

**Growth:** Combine a flexible route with a stable minimum commitment.

**Example:** A project changes direction, and you revise the plan while keeping the original delivery promise visible.

**Practice:** List what can change in your current plan and what must remain dependable.

**Career settings to explore:** Flexible project work, opportunity coordination, varied service roles..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 129–129, B PDF pp. 115–115, C PDF pp. 84–87.

## 11. Strength

**Core:** capacity and measured force. **Themes:** capacity, effort, restraint.

**Constructive:** Apply sustained effort, tolerate frustration, and use influence without intimidation.

**Shadow:** Pushing through every limit, demanding that others match your capacity, or treating rest as failure.

**Talents:** Persistence, demanding execution, motivation, and steady effort.

**Tension:** Wanting to support others while pushing them past their chosen limits.

**Relationships:** Strong feelings can be expressed directly without overpowering another person.

**Communication:** Lower the pressure while keeping the request clear.

**Resources:** Capacity is useful when effort serves a defined result; working harder is not always the appropriate response.

**Family:** Notice whether effort, toughness, or endurance became a condition of acceptance.

**Growth:** Practice restraint as deliberately as effort.

**Example:** You can finish a task yourself but instead help the team set a sustainable pace.

**Practice:** Set both a work commitment and a clear point at which you will stop.

**Career settings to explore:** Execution-intensive work, team leadership, coaching as an exploratory theme..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 130–130, B PDF pp. 116–116, C PDF pp. 88–92.

## 12. The Hanged Man

**Core:** perspective and freely chosen service. **Themes:** perspective, service, boundaries.

**Constructive:** Step outside a habitual viewpoint and help in a way that respects both people's limits.

**Shadow:** Making yourself indispensable through sacrifice, staying indefinitely suspended, or expecting gratitude for unrequested help.

**Talents:** Reframing, creative interpretation, careful support, and service design.

**Tension:** Wanting to be generous while hoping sacrifice will guarantee belonging.

**Relationships:** Offer care freely and allow a refusal without making it a test of love.

**Communication:** Ask permission before helping and state your own limits.

**Resources:** Useful service can be paid fairly; low compensation is not proof of generosity or spiritual value.

**Family:** Look at who was expected to give up needs to keep the family functioning.

**Growth:** Distinguish a chosen contribution from an obligation you never agreed to.

**Example:** You help a friend for an agreed hour rather than silently canceling your own commitments.

**Practice:** Before saying yes, name the request, your available capacity, and your boundary.

**Career settings to explore:** Support services, creative communication, service design..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 131–131, B PDF pp. 117–117, C PDF pp. 93–97.

## 13. Death

**Core:** completion and transition. **Themes:** completion, transition, release.

**Constructive:** Recognize an ending, remove what no longer serves, and make room for the next stage.

**Shadow:** Creating disruption to escape discomfort, cutting ties impulsively, or clinging to something whose role has ended.

**Talents:** Editing, transition work, clearing obsolete processes, and decisive completion.

**Tension:** Wanting renewal while fearing the uncertainty between an ending and a new start.

**Relationships:** Discuss what needs to change before treating the whole relationship as disposable.

**Communication:** Name what is ending and what you want to carry forward.

**Resources:** Changing a method may release resources; the archetype predicts neither loss nor a financial windfall.

**Family:** You may question a repeated pattern without rejecting everyone connected with it.

**Growth:** Finish an ending carefully instead of relying on a dramatic break.

**Example:** You retire an unused service, tell its users clearly, and move the useful parts into a simpler offer.

**Practice:** Identify one obsolete commitment and plan a respectful completion.

**Career settings to explore:** Change management, editing, process renewal..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 132–132, B PDF pp. 118–118, C PDF pp. 98–101.

## 14. Temperance

**Core:** integration and proportion. **Themes:** integration, proportion, patience.

**Constructive:** Combine different elements thoughtfully and choose a pace that allows quality to develop.

**Shadow:** Avoiding necessary decisions in the name of peace, suppressing a preference, or swinging between restriction and excess.

**Talents:** Refinement, creative integration, patient craft, and finding workable proportions.

**Tension:** Wanting calm while avoiding the direct discussion needed to create it.

**Relationships:** Make room for different rhythms without asking either person to disappear.

**Communication:** Translate competing needs into a practical compromise.

**Resources:** A sustainable balance between use and reserve can matter more than a dramatic result.

**Family:** Notice how disagreement was moderated and whether anyone's preference was lost.

**Growth:** Choose a workable middle path without making moderation another rigid rule.

**Example:** You combine two conflicting project requirements into a smaller design that satisfies the essential parts.

**Practice:** Adjust one routine in a small way and review whether it actually helps.

**Career settings to explore:** Craft, creative production, process refinement..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 133–133, B PDF pp. 119–120, C PDF pp. 102–104.

## 15. The Devil

**Core:** desire and the use of power. **Themes:** desire, incentives, power.

**Constructive:** Recognize ambition, attraction, and incentives honestly, then choose how to use them responsibly.

**Shadow:** Manipulating a need for approval, letting a reward dictate your choices, or pretending a strong desire does not exist.

**Talents:** Negotiation, seeing incentives, commercial persuasion, and confronting uncomfortable motives.

**Tension:** Wanting strong experiences while wanting to remain independent of their rewards.

**Relationships:** Attraction benefits from explicit consent and freedom to say no.

**Communication:** Name the incentive or power imbalance instead of using it covertly.

**Resources:** A profitable offer still needs fair terms; desire for a result can distort your assessment of the cost.

**Family:** Notice bargains in which affection or approval seemed conditional on compliance.

**Growth:** Admit what you want and decide what you will not do to obtain it.

**Example:** You recognize that a commission is influencing your recommendation and disclose the conflict.

**Practice:** Write down a desired reward, its real cost, and one boundary you will keep.

**Career settings to explore:** Negotiation, sales, commercial strategy..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 134–135, B PDF pp. 121–121, C PDF pp. 105–108.

## 16. The Tower

**Tension:** Wanting certainty while resisting the review that would make it more realistic.

**Relationships:** Challenge an unhealthy agreement without using the conflict to humiliate the other person.

**Resources:** Examine how a plan works under pressure; the archetype is not a prediction of bankruptcy or disaster.

**Family:** Question a rigid family story without claiming the chart reveals a hidden catastrophe.

**Practice:** Choose one strong assumption in your plan and test it against available facts.

**Career settings to explore:** Process redesign, building and infrastructure as symbolic examples, organizational renewal..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 136–136, B PDF pp. 122–122, C PDF pp. 109–113.

**Family:** Explore identities beyond the family while keeping the connections you actually value.

**Career settings to explore:** International coordination, languages, systems integration..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 141–141, B PDF pp. 132–132, C PDF pp. 130–133.

## 22. The Fool

**Core:** openness and responsible freedom. **Themes:** freedom, play, experimentation.

**Constructive:** Try a fresh approach, stay curious, and leave room for play without abandoning consequences.

**Shadow:** Confusing freedom with exemption from commitments, drifting, or trusting an appealing possibility without checking it.

**Talents:** Improvisation, experimentation, play, and generating fresh alternatives.

**Tension:** Wanting no restrictions while depending on stable support from others.

**Relationships:** Negotiate room for independence while keeping commitments visible.

**Communication:** Say when you are exploring an idea rather than making a promise.

**Resources:** A small experiment can contain uncertainty; borrowing or gambling is not justified by this archetype.

**Family:** You can question an inherited script without pretending that actions have no effects on others.

**Growth:** Define the boundaries that make exploration sustainable.

**Example:** You test a new activity with a limited budget and keep your existing commitments intact.

**Practice:** Design a small experiment with a clear limit on time, money, and promises.

**Career settings to explore:** Experimental creative work, entertainment, varied project roles..

**Sources:** A PDF pp. 142–143, B PDF pp. 133–135, C PDF pp. 134–138.
